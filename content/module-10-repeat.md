## Objectives

- Execute the conversion from primary residence to rental cleanly
- Choose between second-tier entitlement, one-time restoration, and sale
- Qualify for VA loan #2 while holding property #1
- Use IRRRL and VA cash-out refinances at the right moments

## The cycle, stated precisely

```
Buy (VA, 0 down, 1–4 units)
  → Occupy genuinely
    → Stabilize: leases in place, books clean, reserves funded
      → Life event triggers a move (PCS, job, family)
        → Convert to rental: insurance, licensing, taxes, servicer address
          → Choose restoration path
            → Qualify for loan #2 with rental income now supporting you
              → Buy again
```

The cycle length is typically 18 months to 3 years. Four cycles is a portfolio.

## Step 1 — Convert the property properly

The night you move out, run this list (it repeats Module 8 because it is the list people skip):

- Convert to a **landlord / dwelling fire policy** with adequate liability limits
- Consider an **umbrella policy** once you own more than one property
- **Remove owner-occupancy tax exemptions** where required
- **Register for rental licensing / inspection** if your city requires it
- **Update the servicer** with your mailing address
- **Set the rent correctly.** You are no longer a resident who wants nice neighbors; you are an owner who needs market rent. Pull comparable listings.
- Decide **self-manage or hire** — and if you are moving far away, hire

:::tip Have the lease signed before you leave
A signed lease with rent starting the week you vacate turns a scary transition into a boring one. Start marketing the unit 45–60 days before your move date. An empty unit during the transition is the single most common cash-flow shock in the first cycle.
:::

## Step 2 — Choose your restoration path

| Path | Keeps property? | Entitlement result | Best when |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Second-tier (bonus) entitlement** | Yes | Partial; county limits apply | Remaining entitlement covers the next purchase with little or no down |
| **One-time restoration** (pay VA loan in full, keep property) | Yes | Full entitlement restored — **once** | The next purchase is expensive and second-tier is not enough |
| **Sell and restore** | No | Full entitlement | You want the equity out, or the property was a mistake |
| **Substitution of entitlement** | No (buyer assumes) | Full entitlement | Your loan has a low rate and a veteran buyer wants it |

Run the numbers in the [second-tier calculator](module-02-entitlement-math.html) before you decide. In most mid-priced markets, second-tier entitlement alone supports purchase #2 with zero or minimal down — save the one-time restoration for when you actually need it.

## Step 3 — Qualify for loan #2

Your file has changed since last time, mostly for the better:

**What helps you now**

- Documented landlord experience — frequently the key that unlocks counting rental income
- Rental income on Schedule E, once seasoned
- Equity, reserves, and a longer credit history
- A lender relationship and a track record of closing

**What hurts you**

- The first mortgage now counts in your DTI — unless the rental income offsets it
- Reserve requirements typically increase with each financed property
- Some lenders limit the number of financed properties they will lend on

**The critical question to ask your lender:** *"To offset the payment on my departing residence with its rental income, what exactly do you require?"* Answers vary: a signed lease plus proof of the security deposit, 25–30% equity in the departing residence, two years of landlord history, or some combination. **Know the answer before you move out**, because some requirements (like an appraisal showing equity) take time to satisfy.

## Step 4 — The refinance tools

### IRRRL (Interest Rate Reduction Refinance Loan)

The VA streamline. Refinances an existing VA loan into a lower rate with minimal documentation — usually no new appraisal, no new income verification, and a **0.50% funding fee**.

Crucially for this strategy: **you do not have to currently occupy the property to do an IRRRL.** You only need to certify that you *previously* occupied it. That makes the IRRRL the tool for lowering the rate on a rental you once lived in — directly improving its cash flow.

Requirements and limits: the new loan must generally lower your rate (or move from ARM to fixed), the loan must be current, a net tangible benefit test applies, and cash back is limited (typically capped at $6,000 for energy improvements). It does not restore entitlement — the entitlement stays charged to the same property.

### VA cash-out refinance

Replaces a VA or non-VA loan with a VA loan and takes equity out. VA rules permit up to 100% LTV, though **most lenders cap at 90%**. Funding fee is 2.15% first use / 3.30% subsequent. Occupancy is required — this is a primary-residence product, so you use it on the property you currently live in, not on a rental.

Use it carefully. Pulling equity to fund the next down payment is a legitimate move, but it raises the payment on the property you pulled from and can push a cash-flowing rental into the red.

### Refinance into conventional to trigger one-time restoration

The advanced play. Refinance your VA-financed rental into a **conventional** loan. The VA loan is paid in full, the property stays yours, and you apply for the one-time restoration — full entitlement, back in your hands, with the rental still in the portfolio.

Conditions to check first:

- Do you have the 20–25% equity a conventional investment-property refinance typically requires?
- Does the property's DSCR support the new loan?
- Is the new rate acceptable? Investment-property conventional rates price above owner-occupied.
- Have you already used your one-time restoration? It is once, ever.

:::money Sequencing the whole engine
Cycle 1: buy a duplex with full entitlement, $0 down.
Cycle 2: PCS. Rent the duplex. Buy a triplex with second-tier entitlement, minimal down.
Cycle 3: refinance the duplex into conventional, use one-time restoration, buy a fourplex with restored full entitlement, $0 down.
Cycle 4: out of VA entitlement — Module 11's conventional and DSCR financing, funded by the cash flow and equity from the first three.

Nine doors. Roughly four to eight years. Down payment capital deployed: close to nothing on the first three.
:::

## Knowledge check

<details class="toc"><summary>Can you IRRRL a property you no longer live in?</summary><p>Yes. The IRRRL requires only that you certify you previously occupied the property, which makes it the standard tool for lowering the rate on a former residence now operating as a rental.</p></details>
<details class="toc"><summary>You want full entitlement back but do not want to sell. What do you do?</summary><p>Refinance the VA loan into a conventional loan so the VA loan is paid in full, then apply for the one-time restoration of entitlement. It can only be used once.</p></details>
<details class="toc"><summary>Why ask about departing-residence rental income before you move out?</summary><p>Because some lender requirements — an appraisal proving 25–30% equity, a seasoned lease, documented landlord history — take time or must be established before the move. Discovering the requirement after you have already relocated can delay purchase #2 by months.</p></details>
