## Objectives

- Structure an offer that gets closing costs paid by someone other than you
- Know which fees a VA borrower may not pay, and who pays them instead
- Navigate the VA appraisal, Tidewater, and Reconsideration of Value
- Run the closing timeline without losing the deal

## The real "little to no money down"

Zero down payment does not mean zero cash. The costs of buying are:

| Cost | Typical range | Who can pay it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Down payment | $0 with entitlement | You (usually $0) |
| VA funding fee | 0–3.3% of loan | **Financed into the loan** |
| Lender fees (origination, underwriting) | $1,000–$3,500 | You, seller, or lender credit |
| Appraisal | $600–$1,400 (multi-unit higher) | You, seller, or lender credit |
| Title, escrow, recording | $1,500–$4,000 | You, seller, or lender credit |
| Prepaid interest, taxes, insurance, escrow setup | $3,000–$9,000 | You, seller, or lender credit |
| Inspections | $500–$1,500 | **You** (paid up front, not at closing) |
| Earnest money | 1–3% of price | You — **returned to you at closing** |

The four levers that get your cash-to-close near zero:

1. **Finance the funding fee.** Standard practice; it rides on top of the loan.
2. **Seller-paid closing costs.** Ask for them in the offer.
3. **Lender credits.** Accept a slightly higher rate in exchange for the lender paying your costs.
4. **Earnest money return.** Your earnest deposit is credited back to you at closing when the seller is covering costs — so the money you fronted comes home.

:::money What the VA allows a seller to pay
A VA seller may pay **all of the buyer's customary loan closing costs** — and, on top of that, **concessions of up to 4% of the property's established reasonable value** for items like prepaid taxes and insurance, paying off the buyer's judgments or debts, gifting appliances, or paying the funding fee. The 4% cap applies to concessions, not to ordinary closing costs.

This is why the VA buyer's classic offer is not "I'll pay less" but **"I'll pay your price if you pay my costs."** Confirm the current rules with your lender — concession treatment is technical and lender interpretations vary.
:::

## Non-allowable fees

The VA prohibits the veteran from paying certain fees. Commonly non-allowable to the borrower:

- Lender's attorney fees
- Escrow or settlement fees in many jurisdictions
- Document preparation fees
- Loan application or processing fees charged separately
- Brokerage or buyer-broker commission in certain structures
- Termite/pest inspection in many states (traditionally seller-paid; rules have shifted — confirm locally)

These are usually covered by the lender's flat 1% origination charge or paid by the seller. Your Loan Estimate should show them handled. **If your closing disclosure has you paying a non-allowable fee, flag it before signing.**

## Structuring the offer

Three offers on the same $500,000 duplex:

| Offer | Price | Seller pays | Net to seller | Your cash to close |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | $485,000 | $0 | $485,000 | ~$13,000 |
| B | $500,000 | $13,000 | $487,000 | ~$0 |
| C | $508,000 | $18,000 | $490,000 | ~$0, plus a rate buydown |

Offer B nets the seller **more** than Offer A while costing you nothing at the table. Sellers and their agents often evaluate offers on headline price. Use that.

:::warning The appraisal is the ceiling
You cannot inflate the price beyond appraised value and expect the loan to fund — the VA lends against the **Notice of Value**, not the contract. Offer C only works if the property appraises at $508,000. Ask your agent to support the price with comparable sales before you write it.
:::

## The VA appraisal

The VA assigns an independent appraiser from its own panel. Your lender cannot pick one. The appraisal produces a **Notice of Value (NOV)** and a list of any repair conditions required to meet MPRs.

Key features:

- **It is not an inspection.** Get your own inspection. The appraiser is protecting the VA's guaranty, not your investment.
- **Multi-unit appraisals** include a rent schedule (Form 1007 or 1025), which your lender may need to count rental income.
- **Timelines vary** by region and appraiser availability. Build realistic timeframes into the contract.

### Tidewater

If the appraiser is likely to come in below the contract price, the **Tidewater Initiative** gives your lender and agent a short window (typically two business days) to submit additional comparable sales before the value is finalized. When your agent gets a Tidewater notice, respond fast and with real comps. This is a genuine second chance and many agents waste it.

### Reconsideration of Value

If the NOV comes in low anyway, you can request a **Reconsideration of Value** through the lender, supported by comparable sales the appraiser missed or factual errors in the report. It is not an appeal on the basis of disappointment; it needs evidence.

### The VA Amendatory Escape Clause

Every VA purchase contract includes it. If the property appraises below the contract price, **you may walk away and recover your earnest money**, even without an appraisal contingency. You cannot waive it, and no seller can require you to. Your options on a low appraisal are: renegotiate the price to value, pay the difference in cash (allowed), or terminate.

## Inspections on a multi-unit

Spend the money. Order:

- **General home inspection** — insist the inspector enters *every* unit; coordinate tenant access early
- **Sewer scope** — on anything pre-1980; a sewer lateral is a $10,000–$25,000 surprise
- **Roof inspection** by a roofer, not just the general inspector
- **Electrical panel evaluation** on older buildings (Federal Pacific, Zinsco, aluminum branch wiring — insurance issues)
- **WDI/termite** where applicable
- **Radon and lead paint** for pre-1978 buildings
- **HVAC service records** for each unit's system

Use findings as a second negotiation. A clean inspection means you paid the right price; a bad one means you get a credit, a repair, or an exit.

## Tenant-occupied purchases

If units are occupied, additional diligence:

- Obtain **estoppel certificates** from every tenant
- Confirm **security deposits transfer** at closing and are accounted for exactly; in many states mishandling deposits creates statutory penalties
- Review leases for term, renewal, and any concessions the seller promised
- Understand that in many jurisdictions **you inherit the leases** — you cannot simply remove a tenant to move in. If you need a specific unit vacant to satisfy occupancy, confirm the legal path to vacancy **before** you go under contract.

:::warning This is the most common way VA multi-unit deals break
A veteran buys a fully occupied fourplex and then discovers that the unit they intended to live in has a tenant with nine months left on a lease and local law does not permit an owner-move-in termination. Now they cannot occupy — and occupancy is a loan certification. Solve this in the contract: make seller delivery of a vacant unit at closing a condition of the purchase, or buy a property that already has a vacancy.
:::

## Closing timeline

| Days | Milestone |
| --- | --- |
| 0 | Offer accepted; deliver earnest money; send contract to lender |
| 1–3 | Lender orders VA appraisal and opens processing; schedule inspections |
| 5–10 | Inspections complete; negotiate repairs or credits |
| 7–21 | Appraisal delivered; respond to Tidewater if it comes |
| 10–25 | Underwriting conditions; do not open new credit or change jobs |
| 20–30 | MPR repairs completed and re-inspected if required |
| 25–35 | Clear to close; Closing Disclosure issued (3-business-day rule) |
| 30–45 | Final walkthrough of **every unit**; sign; fund; record |

:::tip During escrow, freeze your financial life
No new credit cards, no car purchases, no large unexplained deposits, no job changes, no closing accounts. Lenders re-pull credit and re-verify employment days before closing. Furniture for the new place gets bought after funding.
:::
