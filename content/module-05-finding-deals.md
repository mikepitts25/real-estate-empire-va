## Objectives

- Evaluate markets against the constraints unique to a VA house hack
- Hire an agent and build a sourcing system
- Read a rent roll and spot the numbers that are being hidden from you
- Run a deal-flow process with a target of offers per month, not listings per month

## Choose the market you can legally live in

Every VA purchase must be somewhere you will genuinely occupy. That narrows the map to where you work, where you are stationed, or where you are moving. Within that constraint, evaluate submarkets on:

- **Rent-to-price ratio.** Where does $1 of price buy the most rent? Older near-in neighborhoods and small multifamily corridors usually beat new subdivisions.
- **Multi-unit inventory.** Some metros have thousands of duplexes; some have almost none. Search the MLS for 2–4 unit sales in the last 12 months before you commit to a market.
- **Landlord-tenant law.** Eviction timelines and rent regulations vary enormously by state and city. A 9-month eviction process changes your vacancy assumption.
- **Employment and population trend.** Diversified employment beats a single large employer. Military towns have reliable demand but are exposed to base decisions.
- **Property tax regime.** Check whether taxes reassess at sale price, and whether owner-occupancy exemptions apply to multi-unit properties.
- **Insurance cost and availability.** In coastal and wildfire markets this has become a deal-level variable, not a rounding error.

:::tip PCS is an opportunity, not a disruption
Every permanent change of station is a chance to add a property. Service members who buy a small multi-unit at each duty station and keep them accumulate portfolios faster than almost anyone — the moves that make civilian investing hard are the exact mechanism that makes this strategy work.
:::

## Hire the right agent

Interview three. The one you want has closed 2–4 unit deals and has personally handled a VA transaction in the last year.

Ask:

- "How many 2–4 unit properties have you closed in the last 24 months?"
- "How many VA-financed transactions have you represented in the last year?"
- "How do you handle a listing agent who says they won't accept VA offers?"
- "Will you set me up with an MLS auto-search including 2–4 unit and multifamily property types?"
- "Can you pull the last 12 months of small multifamily sales in these three submarkets?"
- "Do you own rental property yourself?"

Red flags: pushing you toward a single-family home "because multis are complicated"; not knowing what an MPR is; treating your VA loan as a problem to apologize for.

## Where deals come from

1. **The MLS, with alerts set correctly.** Most agents forget to include the multifamily property class. Verify the search yourself.
2. **Days-on-market filters.** Properties at 45+ days with a price reduction are where negotiating leverage lives — especially for seller-paid closing costs (Module 7).
3. **Failed listings.** Expired and withdrawn multi-unit listings, contacted politely, are a reliable source.
4. **Tired landlords.** Owners of small multis who are done managing. Reachable via county assessor records and mailers.
5. **Small local wholesalers.** Useful, but most wholesale deals are distressed and will fail VA MPRs — screen hard.
6. **Word of mouth in veteran networks.** Local VFW, unit networks, veteran real estate groups, and base housing offices know about properties before the market does.
7. **"For rent" signs on multi-units.** An owner advertising a vacancy is an owner you can talk to.

:::warning Watch the VA-unfriendly listing myth
Some listing agents still believe VA buyers are slow, fussy, or likely to fall out. Counter it with facts in the offer packet: a fully underwritten pre-approval letter, your lender's direct phone number, an average close-time figure, and a cover letter from your agent. Do not respond by waiving the appraisal — the VA Amendatory Clause (Module 7) cannot be waived anyway.
:::

## Reading a rent roll honestly

When you receive a rent roll and operating statement, treat them as marketing until verified.

| What they show | What to verify |
| --- | --- |
| "Market rent $1,600/unit" | Actual signed leases and 12 months of bank deposits. Proforma rent is a wish. |
| "Expenses: $4,200/yr" | Tax bill, insurance declaration page, 24 months of utility bills, and any management contract |
| "Fully occupied" | Estoppel certificates from each tenant confirming rent, deposit, and lease term |
| "Tenants pay all utilities" | Check the meters. One meter for four units means you pay. |
| "Recent updates" | Permits at the building department. Unpermitted work is your problem after closing. |

Ask for: current leases, tenant ledgers, security deposit accounting, the last two years of tax returns or Schedule E for the property, utility bills, and a list of personal property conveying.

## Screening at speed

You will look at far more properties than you buy. Build a funnel and keep the numbers.

```
100 listings reviewed
 →  25 pass the buy box on price and rent
 →  10 analyzed fully in the House Hack Analyzer
 →   5 toured
 →   3 offers written
 →   1 accepted
```

The single biggest predictor of whether someone completes deal #1 is **offers written per month**. Analyzing is not progress; touring is not progress. Offers are progress. Set a quota of two to four written offers per month and hold yourself to it.

## Property condition and MPRs

The VA requires the property to be safe, structurally sound, and sanitary — **Minimum Property Requirements**. Common MPR failures on older small multifamily:

- Peeling paint on pre-1978 buildings (lead-based paint hazard)
- Non-functioning heat in any unit, or no permanent heat source
- Active roof leaks or a roof at the end of life
- Missing handrails, unsafe stairs, broken windows
- Exposed wiring, unpermitted panels, knob-and-tube
- Standing water in crawlspaces, active pest infestation, failing septic
- No functioning kitchen or bath in a unit
- Wood-destroying insect damage (many states require a WDI inspection)

Cosmetic ugliness is fine — dated kitchens, bad carpet, awful paint colors are opportunity. **Systems failures are the problem.** If a property needs a roof, either negotiate for the seller to replace it before closing, or look at the VA renovation loan (Module 9).
