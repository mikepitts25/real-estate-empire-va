## Objectives

- Compare the house hack formats available under a VA loan and pick yours
- Model the two economic states of a house hack: while you live there, and after you leave
- Understand why the first deal is the hardest and the most important
- Set a buy box you can actually execute

## Why this is the whole program

A conventional investor saves $80,000, buys a $400,000 rental, and earns maybe $300/month. You buy the same building with $0 down, live in it, eliminate your own rent, and the property's economics improve every time you leave one and buy the next.

Your advantage is not that you are smarter. It is that **the down payment — the constraint that stops almost everyone — does not apply to you.** The strategy converts that structural advantage into doors.

## The formats

| Format | Down | Complexity | Cash flow after you leave | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Fourplex** | $0 | High | Best | Hardest to qualify for and find; the strongest engine |
| **Triplex** | $0 | Medium-high | Strong | Often overlooked; less competition than duplexes |
| **Duplex** | $0 | Medium | Good | The realistic first deal in most markets |
| **SFH + ADU / guest house** | $0 | Medium | Good | Check zoning and legal-use status before you rely on ADU rent |
| **SFH + roommates** | $0 | Low | Weak | Easiest to buy, worst engine — rent stops when you move out |
| **SFH + basement conversion** | $0 | Medium | Medium | Permits matter; unpermitted "units" are a lending and insurance problem |

:::money The four-unit ceiling is a gift
The VA lets you finance up to four units with the same zero-down terms as a single-family home. A fourplex at $600,000 with three units renting at $1,500 each produces $4,500/month against a payment that might be $4,200 — and you live there. There is no other mainstream loan product in the country that does this.
:::

## The two states of a house hack

Model every deal twice.

**State 1 — while you occupy.** Only the other units pay. Your goal is that your *effective housing cost* — payment minus the net rent from the other units — is far below what you would pay to rent. If it is zero or negative, the property is paying you to live there.

**State 2 — after you move out.** All units rent. Your goal is positive cash flow after real expense reserves. This is the number that determines whether the property becomes an asset or an anchor once you move on to deal #2.

A deal that looks great in State 1 and loses $400/month in State 2 is not an empire building block; it is a subsidized apartment. Buy for State 2.

## The expense reserves people forget

New investors compute "rent minus mortgage" and call it cash flow. It is not. Budget, as percentages of gross rent:

- **Vacancy: 5–8%** (higher for lower-quality tenancy or transient markets)
- **Maintenance: 5–10%** (higher for older buildings)
- **Capital expenditures: 5–10%** — roof, HVAC, water heater, parking lot. These are not maintenance; they are big, certain, and periodic
- **Property management: 8–10%** — budget it even if you self-manage, because eventually you will not

That is 23–38% of gross rent, before the mortgage. If you skip these lines, your deal analysis is fiction.

## Reading the outputs

- **Housing cost while living there.** Compare it to your current rent or BAH. Negative is excellent, near-zero is a win, and anything below half your current rent is a strong deal.
- **Cash flow after you move out.** This must be positive at realistic rents. If it is thin, ask what happens at 10% lower rents.
- **Cash-on-cash return.** With zero down and seller-paid closing costs, cash to close approaches zero and this number goes meaningless-high. That is a feature of the strategy, not a reason to skip the other metrics.
- **Rent-to-price ratio.** Total gross rent ÷ price. The old "1% rule" is unattainable in most markets today; 0.7–0.9% on a multi-unit is a workable modern screen. Below 0.5% you are buying appreciation, not cash flow — know which bet you are making.

## The first deal is the hardest

Expect this, so it does not discourage you:

- You may not be able to count the subject property's rent (Module 3), so you qualify on your income alone
- Reserves for a 3–4 unit take time to build
- Multi-unit inventory is thin, and investors compete for it with cash
- Your agent probably has not done this before

**Therefore: do not insist on a fourplex for deal #1.** A duplex you can actually close beats a fourplex you cannot. Once you own one rental and have twelve months of landlord history, the second deal gets structurally easier — you have experience, rental income on your tax returns, and equity.

:::mission The realistic first-deal target
A duplex or triplex, in a market you can live in, priced where your effective housing cost drops by at least $800/month versus renting, and which cash flows at least slightly positive once fully rented. Not glamorous. It is the door into everything else.
:::

## Setting your buy box

Write this down before you look at a single listing. A buy box stops you from falling in love with the wrong building.

```
Market / submarkets:  ______________________
Property type:        2–4 units (or SFH + legal ADU)
Price range:          $______ to $______
Minimum total rent:   $______/mo
Max effective housing cost while occupying: $______/mo
Min cash flow once fully rented:            $______/mo
Condition:            must pass VA MPRs; cosmetic work OK, structural no
Max commute / distance from work:           ______
Deal breakers:        ______________________
```
