## Objectives

- Build an operating statement from raw listing data
- Compute NOI, cap rate, cash-on-cash return, and DSCR
- Find break-even occupancy and run stress tests
- Decide with a rule, not a feeling

## The operating statement

Every deal reduces to this structure. Learn it once; use it forever.

```
  Gross Scheduled Rent          all units at market rent, 12 months
− Vacancy & credit loss         5–8% typical
= Effective Gross Income (EGI)
− Operating expenses            taxes, insurance, maintenance, CapEx reserve,
                                management, owner-paid utilities, lawn/snow,
                                licensing, legal/accounting, turnover
= Net Operating Income (NOI)
− Debt service (P&I)
= Cash Flow Before Taxes
```

**NOI excludes the mortgage.** That is deliberate: NOI describes the property, debt service describes your financing. Two buyers of the same building have the same NOI and different cash flow.

## The metrics that matter

| Metric | Formula | What it tells you | Rough target |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Cap rate** | NOI ÷ price | The property's unlevered yield; lets you compare buildings | Compare to local sales, not a national number |
| **Cash-on-cash** | Annual cash flow ÷ cash invested | Return on the money you actually put in | 8%+ conventionally; near-infinite at $0 down |
| **DSCR** | NOI ÷ annual debt service | Can the property carry its own loan? | 1.25+ is healthy; under 1.0 means it cannot |
| **Break-even occupancy** | (Op ex + debt service) ÷ gross rent | How empty can it get before you feed it? | Under 80% is comfortable |
| **Rent-to-price** | Annual gross rent ÷ price | Fast screen | 0.7–0.9%/mo total is workable today |
| **Price per unit** | Price ÷ units | Comparison across buildings | Versus local comps |

:::money Why DSCR matters before you need it
Once you move out and want to refinance into a non-VA loan (Module 11), a DSCR lender will ask exactly this question and will not care about your W-2. Buying properties that clear 1.25 DSCR at today's rates means your exit financing exists. Buying properties that only work because you live in one means it does not.
:::

## A worked deal

A triplex, $525,000. Units rent for $1,450, $1,400, and $1,350 — total $4,200/month, $50,400/year. Taxes $6,300, insurance $2,600, water/sewer paid by owner $2,400, lawn and snow $1,200. You will occupy the largest unit for 18 months.

```
Gross scheduled rent                          $50,400
− Vacancy @ 6%                                 −3,024
= EGI                                          47,376

Operating expenses
  Taxes                                          6,300
  Insurance                                      2,600
  Water/sewer                                    2,400
  Lawn & snow                                    1,200
  Maintenance @ 7% of GSR                        3,528
  CapEx reserve @ 7% of GSR                      3,528
  Management @ 8% of EGI                         3,790
  Licensing, legal, accounting                     600
  Total operating expenses                      23,946

NOI = 47,376 − 23,946 =                        $23,430
Cap rate = 23,430 ÷ 525,000 =                    4.46%
```

Financing: $525,000 at 0% down, 2.15% funding fee financed = $536,288 loan at 6.25%, 30 years → P&I ≈ $3,302/month, $39,624/year.

```
Cash flow (fully rented) = 23,430 − 39,624 = −$16,194/yr  →  −$1,350/mo
DSCR = 23,430 ÷ 39,624 = 0.59
```

**This deal does not work as a rental.** While you live there it may feel fine — you collect $2,850 from the other two units against a $3,900 PITI, so your housing costs $1,050/month instead of the $1,900 you pay to rent. That is a real $850/month improvement. But the day you move out, this property bleeds $1,350/month, and no lender will refinance it on its own income.

This is the most important example in the program: **a good house hack and a good rental are not the same thing, and only the second one scales.**

### What would fix it?

| Change | Effect on cash flow |
| --- | --- |
| Buy at $460,000 instead of $525,000 | +$490/mo — price is the most powerful lever |
| Rents at $1,700 each after renovation (+$1,650/mo gross) | +$1,180/mo |
| Tenants pay their own water (submetering) | +$200/mo |
| Rate drops to 5.0%, refinance | +$580/mo |
| Self-manage for the first three years | +$316/mo |

Notice that no single change rescues it. **Price is the lever you control at the table**, which is why Module 7 is about negotiation and not about hoping.

## Stress tests

Run every deal through these before you write the offer:

- **Rents down 10%.** Recession, oversupply, a bad tenant year. Still positive?
- **Vacancy at 15%** for a year — two turnovers in a fourplex.
- **Interest rate +1%** on your exit refinance.
- **Insurance +40%.** This has happened across whole states recently.
- **Taxes reassess at your purchase price.** In many jurisdictions the seller's tax bill is not the one you will get. Ask the assessor.
- **One $12,000 capital event** in year two — roof section, HVAC, sewer lateral.
- **90 days vacant plus $6,000 in legal and repairs** for an eviction.

If two of these simultaneously sink you, the deal is too tight.

## The decision rule

Write your rule down and follow it mechanically. A workable starting rule for deal #1:

1. Effective housing cost while occupying is at least **$700/month below** your current housing cost, **and**
2. Cash flow once fully rented is **≥ $100/unit/month** at actual (not proforma) rents with full reserves, **and**
3. The deal survives at least four of the seven stress tests, **and**
4. The property passes MPRs or the seller will make it pass

If it fails, you pass on the deal. The good news about a rule is that it removes the emotion from the twelfth showing.

:::warning Appreciation is a bonus, never a thesis
Buying negative cash flow because "it will appreciate" converts your portfolio into a bet on a market you do not control, funded by your paycheck. Appreciation and loan paydown will likely be the largest part of your eventual wealth — but only if you survive long enough to collect them. Cash flow is what lets you survive.
:::
