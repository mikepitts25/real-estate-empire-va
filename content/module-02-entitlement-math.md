## Objectives

- Compute your available entitlement from a COE
- Explain full versus partial (second-tier) entitlement and when loan limits apply
- Calculate the maximum zero-down loan available on a second simultaneous purchase
- Identify which restoration path applies to your situation

This module is the mathematical heart of the program. Most veterans use the benefit once because nobody ever explained this page to them.

## The two entitlement buckets

Your COE describes entitlement in dollars, and there are historically two layers:

- **Basic entitlement: $36,000.** A legacy figure from when loan amounts were small. It represents the VA's guaranty on smaller loans.
- **Bonus (Tier 2) entitlement:** an additional amount that brings the total guaranty up to **25% of the applicable county conforming loan limit**.

So if the conforming loan limit in your county is $806,500, your total potential guaranty is 25% of that — $201,625 — of which $36,000 is basic and the rest is bonus.

:::money Conforming loan limits change annually
The FHFA publishes baseline conforming limits each November for the following year (the 2025 baseline was $806,500, with higher limits in high-cost counties). **Use the current year's number for your county** — look it up on the FHFA website or ask your lender. Every figure in this module uses the limit as a variable, not a constant.
:::

## Full entitlement: no loan limit

Since the Blue Water Navy Vietnam Veterans Act took effect on January 1, 2020, **a veteran with full entitlement has no VA loan limit.** The VA will guarantee 25% of a loan of any size a lender is willing to make. Your purchase price is capped by what you can qualify for and what the appraisal supports — not by a county number.

You have full entitlement if:

- You have never used the benefit, **or**
- You previously used it and have since paid the loan in full and sold the property (and filed for restoration), **or**
- You used the one-time restoration and repaid the loan in full

## Partial entitlement: the second-tier calculation

Here is the case that actually matters for empire building: **you still own a home with a VA loan on it, and you want to buy another.** You have *partial* (second-tier, or bonus) entitlement, and county loan limits come back into play.

The formula:

```
Total guaranty capacity   = 25% × county conforming loan limit
Available entitlement     = Total guaranty capacity − entitlement already charged
Max zero-down loan        = Available entitlement × 4
Down payment required     = 25% × (purchase price − max zero-down loan)
```

That last line is the one people miss: if the new purchase exceeds your zero-down capacity, **you do not need 20% down — you need 25% of the overage only.** That is often a very small number.

### Worked example

Sergeant Reyes bought a $300,000 duplex three years ago with a VA loan. The VA charged $75,000 of entitlement (25% of $300,000). She is PCSing and wants to keep the duplex as a rental while buying a fourplex for $520,000 in a county with an $806,500 limit.

```
Total guaranty capacity = 0.25 × $806,500      = $201,625
Entitlement charged on duplex                   = $75,000
Available entitlement   = $201,625 − $75,000    = $126,625
Max zero-down loan      = $126,625 × 4          = $506,500
Purchase price                                  = $520,000
Overage                 = $520,000 − $506,500   = $13,500
Down payment required   = 0.25 × $13,500        = $3,375
```

She buys a $520,000 fourplex — **while keeping her duplex** — for $3,375 down. And in Module 7 you will learn how seller concessions and lender credits can cover the rest of the closing costs.

## Restoring entitlement

There are three paths back to full entitlement:

### 1. Sell and pay off

The clean path. You sell the property, the VA loan is paid in full at closing, and you file **VA Form 26-1880** to restore entitlement. Typically restores everything.

### 2. One-time restoration (keep the property)

**This is the underrated tool.** If you pay the VA loan in full but **keep the property**, you may have your entitlement restored — but only **once**. Veterans use this after refinancing a VA loan into a conventional loan: the VA loan is paid off, the property stays in the portfolio as a rental, and full entitlement comes back for the next zero-down purchase.

Use it strategically. It is a single card in the deck. In general, save it for the situation where second-tier entitlement is not enough to buy what you want.

### 3. Substitution of entitlement

Another eligible veteran assumes your VA loan and substitutes their entitlement for yours, releasing yours. This requires a qualified veteran buyer and lender/VA approval, but it restores your entitlement without paying off the loan — and gives the buyer a below-market assumable rate. In a high-rate market, this can be a genuinely valuable listing feature.

## Multiple VA loans at once

You may hold more than one VA loan simultaneously, subject to available entitlement and lender qualification. This is exactly what second-tier entitlement is for. There is no rule limiting you to one VA loan at a time; the limits are entitlement dollars and your debt-to-income ratio.

## The strategic view

Think of entitlement as a reusable down payment that the government fronts for you. Each cycle you choose how to recharge it:

| Situation | Best tool |
| --- | --- |
| Keeping property #1, buying #2 in a normal-priced county | Second-tier entitlement, small or zero down |
| Keeping property #1, buying a much more expensive #2 | Refinance #1 to conventional, use one-time restoration |
| Selling #1 | Sell-and-restore, full entitlement back |
| Listing #1 with a very low rate | Market the assumption; a veteran buyer can substitute entitlement |
| Out of entitlement entirely | Module 11: conventional, DSCR, portfolio, partnerships |
