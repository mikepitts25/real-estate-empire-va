## Objectives

- State the occupancy requirement precisely
- Identify the recognized exceptions and how they are documented
- Understand what occupancy fraud is and why it ends this strategy permanently
- Build a compliance file that protects you years later

Read this module twice. Everything else in the program is optimization. **This module is the foundation the whole thing rests on**, and the veterans who lose the benefit lose it here.

## The requirement

When you close a VA purchase loan, you sign a certification that **you intend to personally occupy the property as your home.** The standard expectation is occupancy **within a reasonable time after closing — generally 60 days** — and continuing occupancy as your primary residence.

Three things follow:

1. **It is about your genuine intent at the time you sign.** Not your intent six months later. The certification is a statement of present intent, and it must be true when made.
2. **Personally.** With limited exceptions, a spouse or dependent child occupying does not substitute for you.
3. **Primary residence.** Not a second home, not a weekend place, not a property you visit.

There is **no VA rule requiring you to stay for exactly twelve months.** The "one year rule" that circulates online is a simplification — twelve months of documented occupancy is simply strong evidence that your certification was truthful, and many lenders impose their own occupancy expectations by contract. Circumstances genuinely change; the VA's concern is whether your certification was honest when you made it.

:::warning What crosses the line
Buying a property you never intend to live in, and signing the occupancy certification anyway, is **mortgage fraud** — a federal crime under 18 U.S.C. § 1014 and related statutes. Consequences include loss of the benefit, loan acceleration (the entire balance due immediately), civil liability, and criminal prosecution.

Everything in this program assumes you actually live in each property you buy with a VA loan. That is not a legal disclaimer bolted on the side. **It is the strategy.** The strategy works *because* you move in, not in spite of it.
:::

## Recognized exceptions

The VA recognizes real-life situations. All of them require documentation, and most require lender and VA acknowledgment:

- **Deployment or temporary duty.** A service member deployed away from their permanent duty station may satisfy occupancy through a spouse, or through their own prior or intended occupancy, with documentation.
- **Spouse occupancy.** A spouse may satisfy occupancy when the service member cannot due to active duty. A dependent child can satisfy it with an attorney-in-fact certification in certain cases.
- **Delayed occupancy up to 12 months.** In limited circumstances — property repairs, a pending PCS, a documented future retirement date — occupancy beyond 60 days may be acceptable with a specific date certain and lender/VA approval. It is not automatic.
- **Retirement.** A veteran retiring within 12 months may certify intent to occupy at retirement, with a copy of the retirement application and evidence the income supports the loan.
- **Intermittent occupancy for employment.** Some occupations require travel; occupancy is judged on where you maintain your home.

If you need an exception, **get it in writing from your lender before closing.** Do not rely on an assumption.

## Leaving the property later

You bought, you lived there, and now you are moving — a PCS, a job change, a new baby, a marriage, a separation, a school district. This is normal and permitted. Nothing in the VA rules requires you to sell.

Do these things when you leave:

- **Notify your loan servicer** of your new mailing address (not a "permission" request — it is your loan)
- **Convert your homeowner's policy** to a landlord/dwelling-fire policy with liability coverage. An owner-occupied policy on a rented property can result in a denied claim.
- **Update your property tax status** if you claimed an owner-occupancy exemption — failing to do so is a tax problem
- **Check local rental licensing** and register the unit if required
- **Document why you moved.** Orders, an offer letter, a lease in the new city, a birth certificate. Keep it forever.

:::tip The compliance file
For every property, keep a single folder containing: the closing package, the occupancy certification, proof of occupancy (utility bills in your name, driver's license with that address, voter registration, bank statements, vehicle registration, mail), the reason you left, the date the first tenant lease started, and the landlord insurance policy. If anyone ever asks — a servicer audit, a VA review, a lender several purchases later — you answer in five minutes with documents instead of memory.
:::

## Renting the other units while you live there

This is explicitly contemplated by the program. A 1–4 unit VA purchase where you occupy one unit and rent the others is exactly what the multi-unit allowance is for. Renting spare bedrooms in a single-family home you occupy is likewise fine.

What is **not** fine: renting out the entire property, including "your" unit, while you live somewhere else, in the period when you certified you would occupy.

## Second VA loan while keeping the first

When you use second-tier entitlement for a second VA loan, **the new property must be the one you occupy.** The first property becomes a rental. You cannot hold two VA loans and occupy neither.

Your lender will typically ask for:

- Documentation of the reason for the move (orders, employment)
- The lease and rent for the departing residence, if you want the rental income to count
- Evidence of reserves
- Your COE showing remaining entitlement

Lenders vary on whether they will count rent from the departing residence immediately or require a history. Ask early — it can determine whether you qualify.

## Ethics, plainly

The VA home loan benefit exists because a grateful country decided that people who served should be able to own a home without a down payment. Using it to also build wealth is entirely legitimate; millions of veterans have done exactly that, and the multi-unit allowance was written on purpose.

What erodes the program is the small number of people who treat the occupancy certification as paperwork. Every abuse strengthens the case for lenders adding overlays and Congress tightening rules — which costs the next veteran.

Play it straight. The straight version is already extraordinarily good.
